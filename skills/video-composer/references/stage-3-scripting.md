# Stage 3 — Scripting & Curiosity Architecture

> Scripting is attention architecture. Value (what they learn) is the bricks;
> reason-to-care (why it matters right now) is the mortar. A script of pure
> value with no mortar collapses the moment anything else competes for
> attention.

## 1. The green / purple alternation (master rhythm)

Color-code the script as you write:

- **GREEN — reason to watch.** Problem, question, mystery, obstacle without an
  answer, expectation, stakes, consequence. Opens a loop.
- **PURPLE — answer / payoff.** Solution, reveal, action, explanation. Closes
  a loop.

Rhythm: GREEN → PURPLE → GREEN → PURPLE, continuously. For explainers the
concrete shape is usually Question → Answer → Question → Answer; for
action-led sections it is Setup (why this matters, what could fail) → Action.

Rules:

- Before any teaching block or action block, write 1–2 GREEN sentences.
  Pattern: "Here's the problem with X — if you don't fix it, Y happens" →
  then teach. Never go straight into teaching or doing.
- The viewer must always be able to answer "why am I watching this part?"
  If they cannot, the part is either missing its GREEN lead-in or should be
  cut and summarized in 1–2 voiceover sentences.
- Trimming pauses, tangents, and bad takes is hygiene, not scripting. A clean
  timeline with no GREEN/PURPLE design is still doomed.

Common mistake: delivering boring information instead of answers to the
viewer's questions — because no question was ever raised.

## 2. Four methods to create curiosity (rotate all four)

1. **Mystery / problem / question / obstacle.** Pose something lacking an
   answer. Example shape: an isolated situation with conflicting explanations
   → "so what is its true purpose?" Any stated gap between what is known and
   what matters works.
2. **Counterintuitive / surprising.** State the opposite of expectation
   ("the smaller version is the one to worry about"). Best conversation
   starter; hunt these in research and place them at opens and transitions.
3. **Hidden secret.** Imply someone knows something important and is not
   saying it ("what this industry doesn't tell you about X"). Use only when
   you genuinely deliver non-obvious depth.
4. **Missing component.** Suggest something critical the viewer hasn't
   considered ("you're missing the part that actually decides X"). Must feel
   important — a missing piece about trivia earns no click and no retention.

Keep a running list while scripting; check that each section uses a different
method from its neighbors. Using only method 1 makes the video monotonous.

## 3. The 3-part litmus test (every beat)

Before locking any beat, the viewer must know all three:

1. **What is happening right now?**
2. **Why is it important?**
3. **What could go wrong (or what is at stake if misunderstood)?**

Why: the three together create clarity + stakes + immersion. A viewer who
knows all three watches whatever follows.

Application:

- After drafting, audit each section. If any of the three is missing, add it
  — in an explainer this is usually a voiceover line, not new footage.
- **Lower competence to raise uncertainty.** When a stretch has no natural
  danger, frame the subject as underprepared rather than inflating the task:
  state the task is dangerous or hard for the inexperienced, admit missing
  preparation, name the hostile conditions, and hint failure is likely. A
  journey with uncertain success is as interesting as the destination; the
  same journey presented as safe and inevitable is dead air. This framing can
  be added entirely in voiceover after the fact when the raw material lacks
  stakes.
- If you cannot create curiosity or expectation for a section at all, cut it:
  summarize in a sentence or two and move on. Sunk cost ("we researched /
    filmed a lot") is not a reason to keep a conflict-free stretch.

## 4. Hooks that earn the first 30 seconds

- **Proof, not promise.** Open with the most extraordinary moment or visual
  proof as close to frame one as possible. A promise ("stay till the end to
  see X") retains a fraction of what proof retains. Cold-open with the peak:
  extreme question + immediate action or visual.
- **Broadest question first.** Open with the single most broadly appealing
  question on everyone's mind ("what is actually using X like?"), list a few
  fascinating facts, promise both highs and lows, then dive in. Never open
  with unboxing, specs, or narrow detail only enthusiasts care about.
- **Common pain before lesson.** "I understand your problem" — name the
  embarrassing or boring pain, prove you feel it, then solve. Repeat whenever
  a new solution appears, not just in the intro. This makes the maximum number
  of people care the maximum amount about each answer.
- **Never let them feel they know enough.** Before explaining, state what they
  are missing by not knowing it. Cadence: vivid detail → provocative question
  it raises → next detail. Pair each solution with its "other half you haven't
  seen." Lecturing fact-after-fact with no questions lets the viewer feel
  complete and leave.
- **Delayed mystery with callback.** Introduce a weird detail, object, or
  aside without paying it off; callback minutes later. Unanswered questions
  prevent early exits. Lead intros with 2–3 exceptional details, not setup.

## 5. Flow: but / therefore, never and-then

Replace every `this happened AND THEN this happened AND THEN…` chain with
`we tried X, BUT obstacle got in the way, THEREFORE we did Y, BUT it failed,
SO we tried Z`.

Why: and-then hides causality, feels repetitive, and disorients — the viewer
has no path and finds exit points. But/therefore specifies how one thing
leads to the next and keeps immersion.

Apply at three levels: scenes in a story, topics in an explanation, sentences
in a paragraph. Rewrite every "and" transition to show cause or conflict.
Jumping topics or scenes without an explicit connection is a retention leak.

## 6. Clarity system (the biggest retention lever)

Confusion is the most common retention killer, and you are biased: everything
is clear to you because you have full context. The viewer arrives with zero.

- Revisit every part asking "how could a first-timer misunderstand this?"
  State where you are, what is new, and how it connects to what came before —
  constantly, not once.
- Get an outsider to watch or read before you lock. Every outside pass finds
  a boring or confusing moment you are blind to. Treat this as a required
  step, not a nice-to-have.
- Clear + concise is still not enough for new ideas: a crisp definition is
  forgotten until seen in action. For each abstract concept add:
  1. Concise definition (say it simply, as it is).
  2. Analogy (relate to an everyday experience the viewer already has).
  3. Concrete example (show it working, then name the analogy you just used).
  Abstract becomes vivid; vivid becomes memorable.

## 7. Explainer pacing rules

- **Every spoken thing gets a visual.** Map the script line-by-line to an
  asset before calling the script done. Fast pacing over a static frame loses
  details; visual communication carries maximum value at maximum speed. Use
  zoom and pan to point exactly where to look at each moment.
- **Compress no-conflict stretches.** Parts with no uncertainty, no failure
  chance, and no curiosity go by as fast as possible: months of context in a
  20–30 second montage plus 1–2 voiceover sentences, then back to the
  exciting part. Never show a full process out of sunk cost.
- **Hold a visible progression.** Maintain a ladder the viewer climbs:
  worst-to-best ranking, countdown, escalating difficulty, elimination to one
  winner, explicit goal meter. Random order with no escalation and no finish
  line has no pull.
- **Density over budget.** Compress long arcs (setbacks, wins, funny moments,
  multiple locations) into a tight emotional rollercoaster. Clever density of
  highs, lows, and novelty beats expensive footage.

## 8. Macro-template for mechanism explainers

For lessons that teach how a system works, use Theory → Paired demo →
Playbook:

1. **Theory.** State the claim plus an everyday analogy that makes it
   intuitive (a casino, a game, a familiar transaction). Keep it short —
   just enough to load the mental model.
2. **Paired demo.** Tell the same mini-story twice with identical facts:
   first flat (pure chronology, no stakes, no questions — the vending-machine
   version), then looped (stakes, Big Question, head fake, re-hook per
   Stage 4). The contrast isolates structure as the variable and proves the
   lesson by feel, not assertion.
3. **Playbook.** Walk each loop step as a tactic with phrasing patterns plus
   a weak-vs-strong example per step, so the viewer leaves with applicable
   moves rather than admiration.

Why: the viewer feels the difference before being asked to believe it, which
carries skeptical audiences further than claims alone.

## 9. Mid-video energy defense

- Around 60–70%, when energy naturally dips, introduce a second objective, a
  new complication, or a higher rung on the ladder. A fresh goal layers new
  failure possibilities onto the old one and gives a new thing to look forward
  to. Riding one premise past its freshness is a slow leak.
- If a section cannot earn its keep, compress it. The kindest cut is the one
  the viewer never notices.

## Exit checklist

- [ ] Script color-codes GREEN → PURPLE with no teaching/action block missing
  its GREEN lead-in.
- [ ] Each section uses one of the 4 curiosity methods; neighbors differ.
- [ ] Every beat passes the 3-part litmus (what / why-important /
  what-could-go-wrong) or is cut to a 1–2 sentence summary.
- [ ] Hook uses proof-first, broadest-question, and pain-before-lesson; no
  and-then chains remain at scene, topic, or sentence level.
- [ ] Every abstract concept has analogy + example; every spoken line has a
  planned visual; no-conflict stretches are compressed; a visible progression
  ladder runs the full video.
