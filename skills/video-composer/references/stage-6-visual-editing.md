# Stage 6 — Visual Editing (Retention Cut for Explainers)

> Change what is on screen to hold attention; direct where to look to build
> understanding; keep it seamless so immersion never breaks. Variety is
> seasoning — the right dose brings out flavor, too much ruins the dish.

## 1. Experience-first edit density

Decide the dose from the Stage 1 promise, then hold it:

- **Entertainment / stimulation promise:** cut pauses so speech is near-
  constant, switch visuals every few seconds, keep motion always present.
  Three different things to look at per minute is boring here.
- **Learning / clarity promise:** still switch often, but hold a visual while
  it is genuinely interesting — 10 seconds on one clear diagram beats three
  confusing cuts. Never minimize shot duration just to minimize it.

Why: disrupting the expected experience is the fastest exit trigger. Fast
style for a cozy audience ruins trust; slow style for an entertainment
audience bores. Cutting pauses is the most important move for entertainment
and the most harmful for comfort — choose per promise.

## 2. Footage decision tree (explainer priority order)

1. **Motion graphics / animation — first choice for essential-but-boring or
   complicated exposition.** A process that would be slow or confusing as a
   talking head or plain B-roll becomes crystal-clear in seconds animated.
   Any moment where the viewer must understand a mechanism, sequence, or
   comparison to keep watching earns animation.
2. **B-roll — the workhorse.** Separately recorded or sourced footage that
   depicts the words: the viewer sees what you mean instead of just hearing
   it. Clearer, faster, and more interesting than a static frame. Use as much
   as possible.
3. **Short presenter / narrator framing — sparingly.** In faceless work this
   is a stylized avatar, kinetic text, or brief on-camera bridge used in short
   bursts for important lines where a personal anchor helps. Never park on it
   while describing an object or process.
4. **Stock — last resort.** A lazy version of B-roll; acceptable when nothing
   else visualizes the line, but never the default. Stylize (grade, crop,
   motion) so it does not read as filler.

Rule: if you say it, show it. Map every script line to an asset before
locking the edit. Fast talk over a static frame loses details the viewer can
never get back.

## 3. Spice without cutting

When a cut is not needed, add motion or overlay to make static immersive:

- Slow scale/position drift on stills; better, a perspective shift.
- Abrupt zoom-in at the exact moment an important line lands — a visual
  "listen up" signal.
- Subtle shake/wiggle on graphics or text only if it fits the style; skip it
  if it cheapens the look. Taste over trend.

## 4. Captions — correctly, not as filler

Captions exist to make the viewer understand and lean into specific words,
not to jack up visual variety. Filling dead space with captions quickly
becomes obnoxious and wastes the chance for a more engaging visual.

- Caption at most 3 words at a time; large, high-contrast, easy to read at a
  glance.
- Reserve captions for the words that decide comprehension (terms, numbers,
  verdicts, quotes).
- If there is genuinely nothing better to show, captions beat a dead frame —
  but a better visual always beats captions.

## 5. Direct focus — never dump a full image

A plain full-screen image is boring and confusing: the viewer does not know
where to look. Guide the eye every time with one or more of:

1. Animate the most important text or area (highlight pulse).
2. Darken or blur everything around the focus point.
3. Hue-shift for connotation (warm/red = danger/negative, yellow/green =
   positive) — use sparingly and consistently.
4. Circles, arrows, underlines (functional, not decorative — they must point
   at the hook).
5. Glow or outline on the subject.
6. Zoom / pan to the focus point, landing exactly on it.

## 6. Make it look good (cheap polish, high return)

Color grade for consistency, a subtle vignette for focus, faint texture or
particle motion for depth. Polish signals value and extends immersion. Flat,
inconsistent, or log-looking footage reads as low-effort even when the script
is excellent.

## 7. Variety dose control

- **Mood variety — almost always good.** Excitement → worry → joy → unease →
  payoff on rotation. Shifting tone keeps the viewer alert, and contrast
  strengthens each mood.
- **Visual variety overdose — harmful.** Too fast means no time to process:
  the viewer cannot absorb anything, and the stream becomes visual mush. If
  frames blur together on a timeline scrub, slow down or simplify.
- **Modulate hold duration with the mood block.** Cut cadence is part of the
  mood, not just the track: shorter holds and quicker cuts for tension and
  excitement, longer holds for reflection and dread. When the mood block
  changes, change the cut rate with it — a new bed over an unchanged cadence
  reads as a costume change with no performance. Verify per block: does the
  hold length feel the assigned emotion?

Test per addition: what effect does this cut/effect/graphic create, and how
much of it is needed for the perfect experience? If the answer is "it looks
cool", cut it.

## 8. Invisible continuity (and intentional breaks)

Goal: a seamless feed with no rough edges where distraction accumulates and
no point where immersion drops.

- Graphics never magically appear: animate in (move/scale into frame) or
  appear with a motivated sound (pop, shutter) that explains the entrance.
- Eye-trace rule: the focus point of the last frame (a face, a number, a
  subject) must match the focus point of the first frame of the next clip.
  Forcing the eye to jump across the screen every cut is a micro-jolt that
  compounds into fatigue.
- Full-screen transitions bridge hard cuts; use a small consistent set, not a
  sampler of every preset.
- Break continuity on purpose, rarely, to jolt: an abrupt cut or change when
  attention needs a reset. 100% seamless lulls; 100% jarring exhausts.

## Exit checklist

- [ ] Edit density matches the Stage 1 promise (not a copied trend).
- [ ] Footage follows the decision tree; every spoken line has a visual;
  complicated passages use motion graphics.
- [ ] Static frames have drift/perspective/zoom; captions ≤3 words and only
  on comprehension-critical words.
- [ ] Every image directs focus with at least one of the six tools.
- [ ] Grade + vignette + subtle texture applied consistently.
- [ ] Scrub test: no mush stretches, no 2-minute single-visual deserts.
- [ ] Graphics enter motivated; eye-trace holds across cuts; 1–2 intentional
  jolts placed, not accidental.
