# Stage 5 — Voiceover & Delivery (TTS-Optimized for Faceless)

> In faceless video there is no face to carry flat lines. Emotion must be on
> the page: in word choice, sentence length, punctuation, and order. Write so
> that even a synthetic voice sounds urgent, and a human voice sounds
> unstoppable.

## 1. Write for the ear, not the eye

- Short sentences. Contractions always. One idea per sentence. Read every line
  aloud (or through TTS) — if you stumble, rewrite. Written prose with nested
  clauses dies when spoken.
- Front-load meaning: the important word goes early in the sentence, never
  buried at the end where feeds truncate attention. "The small ones kill —
  not the big ones" beats "It is worth noting that, contrary to expectations,
  the smaller variants are responsible."
- Prefer short, common words with instant meaning. When you need intensity,
  upgrade the verb, not the word count: tested beats visited, survived beats
  stayed, forced beats made. Then stop — fancy words that obscure meaning
  cost more than they add.
- Vary sentence length deliberately: a run of short punches, then one longer
  explanatory sentence, then a punch. Constant length is monotone even before
  anyone speaks.

Common mistake: pasting essay paragraphs into voiceover. If it reads well
silently and fails aloud, it is not a script.

**AI-draft discipline (you are the drafter).** You MUST draft — but never
ship a first pass. Pass 1: speakable draft (short sentences, front-loaded).
Pass 2: aloud/TTS stumble pass — list every stumbled line verbatim plus its
rewrite. Pass 3: cadence pass — mark the sentence-length pattern per beat.
Exit requires the stumble list and fixes shown in your output. "Polished in
my head" fails. Human writers may use AI for vocabulary, shortening, and
flow polish under the same three passes.

## 2. Put contagious emotion on the page

Emotion is contagious: the feeling in the voice becomes the feeling in the
viewer. A flat read makes anything feel boring; a read that sounds like
something important is being shared makes the viewer lean in — even before
they know what it is.

Because TTS cannot improvise enthusiasm, script it explicitly:

- **Excitement:** louder-by-punctuation — exclamations sparingly, stacked
  short sentences, ascending lists ("bigger, faster, and completely unfair").
- **Suspense:** lower-and-slower by construction — short fragments, ellipses
  of meaning (not literal "..."), questions left hanging one beat before the
  answer. "How could this possibly work?" must sit alone as its own line.
- **Emphasis:** anticipation and key-point pauses are structural: put the
  pause before the payoff line, not after. State the setup, break the
  paragraph, then deliver the payoff as its own block.
- **Thought-quote.** Replace emotion labels with quoted inner monologue in
  raw, unfiltered voice: "I thought, oh no, everyone will think I'm an
  idiot" beats "I was disappointed." Never let thoughts sound corporate
  ("this represents a supreme opportunity") — apply the friend-speech test
  to inner monologue specifically: would you actually think in those words?
- **Show feeling on the body.** Never name the feeling alone ("I was
  relieved," "he was anxious") — write the observable behavior instead: lean
  back plus exhale; pen-tapping plus clock-glancing. Test: could the edit
  draw it without hearing the emotion word? If not, rewrite. Body behavior
  is visual; labels are not.
- **Contrast:** alternate energy deliberately across mood blocks (Stage 1):
  excitement → worry → discovery → unease → payoff. Constant intensity is
  white noise; contrast makes each mood hit harder.

Body-language equivalents for faceless: what a face would do must become a
visual move instead — note the intended gesture in brackets next to the line
so the edit implements it:
- Raised eyebrows (interest) → zoom or highlight pulse.
- Squint (key detail) → tight punch-in on the detail.
- Head nod in sync with words (emphasis) → beat-hit or scale pop on the
  stressed word.
- Hands shifting side-to-side (separating ideas) → split-wipe or alternating
  left/right placement of successive graphics.
- Wide arms (big) → full-frame scale cue; pinched fingers (precise) → magnify
  or fine underline.

For unknown creators with no credentials, scripted energy plus specificity
functions as the trust signal: raw enthusiasm convinces a cold viewer that
something worth sharing is here, before any proof arrives. Test: would a
stranger infer "this must be important" from the read alone? If not, raise
the energy and sharpen the specifics — do not add more claims.

## 3. The friend frame (for writers)

Camera shyness has a writing equivalent: writing for an imagined panel of
critics instead of one interested friend. Critic-writing produces stiff,
over-qualified, memorize-every-word prose — technically flawless, lifeless.

- Write to one specific viewer (Stage 1's named person) who wants to know.
  100% of focus on getting the point across, not on sounding impressive.
- Never memorize-trap: do not demand word-perfect delivery. A flawed,
  excited read with small stumbles beats a flawless monotone — and stumbles
  can be cut. For human narration: get comfortable making mistakes on mic;
  self-consciousness ("am I fast enough? pausing right? saying it right?")
  distracts from meaning and flattens everything.
- Self-check: "would I say this to a close friend exactly like this?" If not,
  simplify until yes.
- **Return-character.** Hosted/hybrid videos: withhold self for the first
  ~15 seconds — value first — then let perspective emerge progressively,
  placing small personal moments right after stakes escalation so bonding
  follows arousal. Pure faceless (no host ever): substitute a consistent
  voice-stance (skeptic, myth-buster, ranker) plus one recurring verdict line
  as the "character." Personality-free commodity content builds no
  relationship and does not last — but the fix for faceless is stance, not a
  host.

## 4. TTS-survival rules

- No stage directions, markdown, or parentheticals in voiceover text. Clean
  spoken words only.
- Expand abbreviations, symbols, and numbers into speakable words. Read "48
  hours", not "48h"; "percent", not "%", unless the engine handles it.
- One breath per sentence: if a sentence needs two breaths, split it.
- Place names, technical terms, and coined words: spell phonetically on first
  use if the engine mangles them; test-generate the hardest 5 lines before
  committing to a voice.
- Keep per-scene blocks short (~10 seconds spoken). Long blocks drift out of
  sync with visuals and give the edit no cut points. Scene = one beat = one
  breath group.

## 5. Retention mechanics inside the read

- State the stakes and the failure case before risky or surprising claims
  (Stage 4): "if this step fails, the whole X collapses — and here is why it
  likely fails." Edge-of-seat is written, not performed.
- Convert every "and" chain into but/therefore (Stage 3) at the sentence
  level too. "We tried X, but Y blocked us, therefore we did Z" reads with
  natural stress; "X and Y and Z" reads flat and hides the story.
- End scenes on forward pull, never on summary. The last line of a scene is a
  bridge (Stage 4 re-hook phrasing), not a conclusion.

## Exit checklist

- [ ] Full script reads clean aloud / via TTS with no stumbles; no essay
  clauses remain.
- [ ] Key words front-loaded; verbs upgraded for intensity without obscurity.
- [ ] Emotion (excitement/suspense/emphasis) is structural — sentence length,
  breaks, and order create it even in a flat read.
- [ ] Each line has a noted visual gesture equivalent for the edit.
- [ ] Voiceover text is clean (no formatting/directions), split into short
  per-scene blocks, hardest lines test-generated.
- [ ] No scene ends on summary; every scene ends on forward pull.
