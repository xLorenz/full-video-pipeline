# Stage 1 — Ideation & Audience

> Pick a video that has a viewer before it has a script. Most retention failures
> are ideation failures: a topic nobody was uncertain about, with no stakes, no
> progression, and no packaging angle.

## 1. Start from an audience, not a topic

Define in one paragraph before anything else:

- **Who** is this for? (name the viewer: what they already watch, what
  experience they want on YouTube right now — hang out, be entertained, learn
  fast, feel awe, fix a pain)
- **What do they value?** List 3–5 things this audience demonstrably rewards
  with watch time (not what you wish they valued).
- **What experience are you promising?** One sentence: "After watching, the
  viewer feels ___ and can ___." Every later choice (pacing, music, edit
  density) must serve that promise. Entertainment promises get dense,
  constantly-moving edits. Learning promises get clarity-first edits with
  guided focus. Mixing promises breaks trust.

Why: the recommendation system predicts enjoyability per viewer. A video that
is predicted-most-enjoyable for a specific, understood audience gets pushed;
a video for "everyone" gets pushed to no one.

Common mistake: starting from "I want to make a video about X" with no named
viewer. If you cannot describe the viewer's current desires, you are guessing.

## 2. Study what the audience already rewarded

1. Collect the 10–20 highest-viewed videos in the niche from the last 12–24
   months (not all-time classics — recent taste).
2. For each, extract the underlying value, not the surface style: what
   question did it answer, what fear did it resolve, what spectacle did it
   deliver, what progression did it ride?
3. Compare your premise side by side: where are they more specific, more
   uncertain, more visual, more urgent than you?
4. Close the gap iteratively until your premise beats them on at least one
   axis (clearer payoff, bigger uncertainty, fresher angle, denser payoff
   rate). Do not copy surface style; out-deliver underlying value.

Why: taste is revealed by behavior, not opinion. Views are votes for value.

Common mistake: studying only favorite creators instead of the niche's actual
outliers, or copying editing tricks while missing the premise advantage.

- **Recency edge (newsjack window).** Separate from the 12–24-month study:
  scan what the audience is excited about in the last 24–72 hours. If you can
  research and ship while the conversation is hot, timeliness supplies free
  attention — small operations beat long-lead-time channels here by structure,
  not talent. Cost: compressed research done fast and honestly; arriving after
  the window closes is worse than skipping. Kill trigger: if you cannot ship
  before the conversation moves on, shelve the angle.
- **Cross-domain input diet.** Do not research only direct peers — a peer-only
  diet goes insular and recycles the same tricks while outsiders innovate past
  you. Watch heavily inside your form AND far outside it (other niches,
  short-form, podcasts, other media): import one device at a time. Log per
  idea: "stolen mechanism + source domain." Combinations of two distant inputs
  are candidate angles on their own.
- **Primary-source ops (for original-detail videos).** When the premise needs
  facts nobody has published, bulk-outreach the involved people (DM, email,
  social) before outlining — expect ~80% of script effort to be gathering.
  Two passes: round 1 educates you on what happened; draft to expose gaps;
  round 2 puts meat on the story. Transcribe everything and keyword-search the
  corpus for quotable specifics before locking the outline.

## 3. Premise patterns that carry built-in uncertainty

Prefer premises that arrive with a question, a risk, or a progression. For
explainers, strong starting shapes include:

- **Shared pain → fix.** Name an embarrassing, boring, or costly pain the
  viewer already feels, prove you feel it too, then promise the fix.
  Works because it makes the maximum number of people care the maximum amount
  about the solution.
- **Mystery with clues.** A situation with conflicting explanations or a
  missing piece (a place with strange theories, a result that should be
  impossible, a habit that backfires). Works because the gap demands closure.
- **Counterintuitive inversion.** The smaller threat is deadlier, the lazy
  method wins, the prohibition hides a system. Works because expectation
  violation triggers update-seeking. Hunt these in research; they are the best
  conversation starters.
- **Hidden mechanism.** Someone or something knows a secret: an industry
  practice, a behind-the-scenes incentive, a concealed cause. Works through
  reactance and information-gap — forbidden knowledge feels valuable.
- **Challenge with stakes.** A goal plus an opposing force (clock, competitor,
  difficulty ladder, elimination). Even an explainer can frame as "can I rank
  / test / survive / build X under constraint Y?" Works because conflict
  drives scenes forward and who-wins curiosity is universal.
- **Antagonist / hunter opposition.** A goal plus an active opposer trying to
  stop you — a person, team, rival method, or personified constraint fighting
  back. Works because it manufactures an underdog to root for, a constant
  threat of failure, escalating worry, and creative conquests that each check
  multiple entertainment boxes at once. Run the cycle: underdog position →
  threat cadence (the hunter closes in) → novel escape or solution. Explainer
  port: "method X vs. a skeptical tester actively trying to break it" or a
  myth/misconception cast as the villain the facts must defeat.
- **Competition-ize anything.** Bolt a head-to-head contest onto any flat
  showcase: X vs. X, taste / test / rank everything to one winner, last to
  remain, bracket to a final. Works because who-wins curiosity plus ranking
  conflict turns catalog content into drama. Doubles as a mid-video rescue:
  when a section goes stale, introduce a ranking or elimination rather than
  more exposition.
- **Timer-plus-forfeit conversion.** Impose a short visible countdown on any
  boring task and link expiry to an explicit, visible forfeit — destroyed
  reward, lost prize pool, public penalty. "5 minutes to finish, or the
  reward burns" turns doing into a thriller. Works because the clock creates
  urgency and the forfeit makes failure concrete and watchable. State both
  the clock and the forfeit before action starts, and restate the clock on
  each new loop.
- **Progression ladder.** Cheapest-to-best, worst-to-best, easiest-to-hardest,
  countdown, elimination to one winner. Works because it gives a constant
  "what's next?" pull and a visible finish line. Random order with no
  escalation has no pull.
- **Mixed-valence frame (good *and* bad).** When a story genuinely has
  positives and negatives, frame it as both — never as a single verdict.
  Works because a verdict resolves the question in advance ("this is amazing"
  → nothing left to learn), while a mixed frame opens a distribution question
  — *which* parts are good, which bad, which truly ugly — answerable only by
  watching. Weak: "why X is terrible." Strong: "the good, the bad, and the
  ugly of X." Applies only when you genuinely hold both sides; faked balance
  reads as hedging.
- **Genre thesis templates.** When mystery/challenge shapes don't fit, give
  the video a point via its genre: **review** = claim-test (does it do what
  was promised?) + glaring issues buyers must know + market fit + worth
  verdict, wrapped in one thesis sentence with stacked proof; **essay** =
  context-stack that reframes surface news into a wider non-obvious story or
  paradox (either choice loses); **documentary** = fixed start/end box (rise
  then bust; the question is why) filled with gathered detail, never
  broad-strokes search summaries.

**Immersion sprint (how to find non-obvious angles).** Inversions and
paradoxes are hunted, not waited for: (1) consume maximally on the subject,
(2) rant about it with friends to surface what actually excites you, (3) map
the ecosystem dependencies (who needs whom), (4) state the paradox where
every option loses. Surface recap vs. ecosystem frame is the difference
between commodity and original.

Weak premises: "some thoughts on X", "everything about Y", "my process for Z"
with no pain, no question, no stakes, no ladder. If the premise can be fully
summarized without creating a question, rework it.

## 4. Define stakes early (even for explainers)

Every premise needs an answer to three questions before scripting starts:

1. **Who** is it happening to? A real human the viewer can picture (you, a
   client, a group, a sympathetic subject). Not an abstraction.
2. **What is at risk?** An explicit consequence of failure or ignorance
   (money, time, energy, status, a launch, a decision the viewer is about to
   make). The brain must visualize loss to start predicting.
3. **Why now?** A ticking clock or decision point (within a week beats
   eventually; the call is in 2 hours beats later that day). Urgency
   compresses stakes and makes prediction run faster.

Stakes do not mean life-or-death. They mean personally relevant to your
viewer: "48 hours to fix the budget or burn it all" retains a media buyer
better than a generic apocalypse retains no one.

Two zero-budget stakes templates (no prize money required):

- **Elicit the personal why in the subject's own words.** Script one
  on-camera (or voiceover) line per subject stating why winning matters
  personally — family, pride, insecurity, a debt to repay. A stated human
  reason makes the audience care about the outcome more than any prize
  amount. Explainer port: attach the lesson to a decision cost the viewer
  already feels ("choose wrong and lose a month of work").
- **Social-forfeit bet.** When no prize exists, manufacture stakes with a
  public humiliation or forfeit wager: loser performs a dare on camera, takes
  a public penalty, or lands on a visible scoreboard. State the exact loser
  penalty up front — the reputational risk is real stakes and costs nothing.

Common mistake: high action or dense facts with no stated consequence. Or
stakes that are dramatic but irrelevant to the named viewer.

## 5. Design the experience dose up front

Decide and write down:

- **Edit density target:** entertainment-first promises earn rapid switching
  (new visual every few seconds, pauses removed, constant motion). Clarity-
  or comfort-first promises earn restraint (longer holds, breathing room).
  Cutting pauses is the most important move for entertainment and the most
  harmful move for authenticity — choose per promise, not per trend.
- **Mood arc:** plan 3–5 mood blocks (e.g., excitement → worry → discovery →
  unease → payoff). Constant tone is boring; contrast between moods makes each
  mood hit harder. Shifting tone keeps the viewer alert.
- **Payoff rate:** one vivid payoff (fact, visual, answer, spectacle) at least
  every 30–60 seconds on paper before production. If the outline has a
  2-minute desert, the idea is thin — merge, cut, or add a loop.
- **Drain-rate calibration.** Set the payoff interval from the named viewer's
  attention span, not the default: fast-drain niches (~20 seconds) need key
  points spaced evenly at that clock; slow-drain niches (2–3 minutes) tolerate
  longer builds. Declare the span, then space payoffs and key points to it.
- **Formula-break valve.** After several on-formula videos, greenlight one
  deliberate rule-breaker (unscripted, different pacing or music, unknown
  structure). Define which 2–3 house rules it violates and what is being
  tested — a reset against staleness that doubles as device research.

## Exit checklist (do not advance until all pass)

- [ ] Named viewer + promised experience written in one paragraph.
- [ ] 10+ niche outliers studied; extracted value listed; premise beats them
  on at least one axis.
- [ ] Premise uses at least one strong shape (pain, mystery, inversion,
  secret, challenge, ladder) — not a summary topic.
- [ ] Who / what-at-risk / why-now answerable in one sentence each.
- [ ] Mood arc and payoff rate sketched; no planned 2-minute desert.
